AI Alignment as the Preservation of the Human Moral Layer: Myopic Error and Recomputation Support

_Blind Review Manuscript_

**Abstract**

This paper redefines the problem of AI alignment not as the moralization of AI outputs but as the preservation of the conditions under which the human moral layer can continue to operate under sustained interaction with AI. Morality has conventionally been treated as a content term associated with goodwill or common sense. In contrast, this paper defines morality functionally as an internal adjustment mechanism that incorporates the sustainability of others, relationships, and the community into one's decision-making, thereby reducing the gap between local and global optima prior to coercive intervention.

The paper further argues that many moral failures arise not primarily from malice but from myopic error, that is, reductions in spatial, temporal, and difficulty range through which interdependence drops out of calculation. AI-mediated systems can either support the recomputation needed to counteract such reductions or accelerate optimization within a narrowed range, thereby externalizing and progressively closing the human recomputation circuit.

The paper repositions morality in the age of AI not as moral content to be preached but as a design principle, and formulates the three-layer structure of ethics, morality, and law as control conditions for optimization schemes in general. This framework inverts the question of AI alignment from the moralization of outputs to the preservation of the human basis for judgment.

_Keywords: morality, AI alignment, human autonomy, moral agency, human-AI interaction, myopic error, recomputation support_

Introduction

Current discussions of AI alignment have largely focused on whether AI systems follow human values, return safe and non-harmful outputs, and produce behavior that appears normatively desirable. These concerns are necessary. They are not sufficient. Even if an AI system consistently outputs responses that look coherent, safe, and morally acceptable, the question remains what sustained interaction with such a system does to the human structure of judgment.

Much of the literature on AI ethics and alignment has therefore concentrated on whether AI outputs are safe, non-harmful, and aligned with human values (Floridi et al., 2018; Ouyang et al., 2022). Yet human societies have long depended on a layer of internal adjustment that operates before coercive law is triggered. The problem is that AI is not neutral with respect to this layer. It can support it. It can also replace it, hollow it out, and erode it in ways that remain difficult to detect.

This paper asks whether the moral layer through which human societies have historically reduced friction prior to legal intervention can still function in the age of AI. The question is deeper than asking which values AI should follow, because what is at stake here is not only the content of values but whether humans still retain the conditions for judgment prior to value selection itself.

Morality has conventionally been discussed through terms such as goodwill, sympathy, decency, correctness, manners, and sociality. None of these descriptions, however, adequately explains what morality does. As a result, morality has often remained important yet underdescribed, and therefore insufficiently connected to institutional design or technological design. If an underdescribed concept is carried into AI evaluation and social design, it must first be redescribed functionally.

The position of this paper is clear. Morality is neither a list of good contents nor a beautiful name for values. It is an internal adjustment mechanism through which the sustainability of others, relationships, and the community is incorporated into one’s decision-making so as to reduce the gap between local and global optima before coercive intervention. This formulation connects to social norm theory and institutional accounts of cooperation while redescribing them in terms of an internal mechanism of inclusion (Bicchieri, 2006; Ostrom, 1990).

The argument presupposes a three-layer structure supporting social resilience: ethics as the protection of re-intervention possibility; morality as internal friction reduction before law; and law as the external stopping and adjudication of conflicts that remain. This paper focuses on the second layer in particular and examines how it operates, why it fails, how AI may erode it, and under what conditions AI may instead support it.

The central explanatory move is to describe many moral failures not in terms of malice or personality deficits but in terms of range. Human beings often fail not because they are wholly irrational, but because they act rationally within a narrowed range while failing to include variables that matter spatially, temporally, or in terms of difficulty. This paper calls that structure myopic error.

AI matters here not simply because it can outperform humans on many tasks. It matters because it can either illuminate variables that have fallen outside human range or accelerate optimization within that narrowed range, thereby externalizing recomputation and undermining the moral layer. In this sense, the question of AI alignment must be reversed. The issue is not only whether AI appears moral, but whether AI is designed so as not to replace the human moral layer and so as to preserve the conditions under which that layer can still operate.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 1 clears conceptual ground by examining the limits of conventional understandings of morality. Section 2 presents the three-layer architecture of ethics, morality, and law. Section 3 states the central proposition. Section 4 diagnoses moral failure as myopic error. Sections 5 and 6 analyze how AI may erode the moral layer and how it may instead be designed as support for recomputation. Section 7 distinguishes circuit-preserving morality from circuit-closing normative pressure. Section 8 examines the driving sources of morality together with the problem of cost and inequality. Section 9 proposes evaluation axes and design requirements, including subject-preservability.

The aim of this paper is neither to teach morality to AI nor to preach that humans should simply become more moral. The aim is to preserve and redesign, within technological and institutional conditions, the layer through which human societies have long adjusted themselves before law intervenes. The core of AI alignment, on this view, lies not only in moralizing outputs but in preserving the conditions under which the human moral layer can continue to function.

1\. Limits of Conventional Understandings of Morality

Morality has long been regarded as important, yet its common descriptions remain remarkably vague. It is routinely associated with goodwill, sympathy, common sense, social maturity, correctness, or manners. Under such descriptions, morality becomes something that seems significant without being clearly described in terms of what it does.

This vagueness is not merely semantic inconvenience. The deeper problem is that morality has often been understood as atmosphere rather than function. If morality is only a matter of seeming considerate or properly social, then its role in social systems cannot be described clearly, and what cannot be described cannot readily be designed, evaluated, or institutionalized in the age of AI.

The argument of this section is therefore straightforward: morality must be reconstrued not as a list of good contents but as a function within social systems. The relevant question is not simply what is right, but what morality does.

Goodwill is one common understanding of morality. Yet goodwill is unstable in strength, duration, and range. It may appear as concern for others in one case and as self-satisfaction or emotional reaction in another. Goodwill may motivate morality, but it cannot define morality. What matters is not whether goodwill is present but whether action is connected to the adjustment of interdependence (Smith, 1759/2002; Scanlon, 1998).

Sympathy is another candidate. But sympathy names a psychological texture rather than a social function. It often concerns proximate others, whereas the problems of modern societies include distant others, delayed consequences, institutional externalities, and complex interdependence. The relevant issue is therefore not simply whether others are emotionally felt but whether variables that matter can be reintroduced into judgment (Tomasello, 2016).

Morality is also frequently identified with custom, convention, or common sense. This view has some explanatory power because it recognizes that morality is socially embedded. Yet it cannot explain why the same convention may stabilize social life in one case and operate as repression or exclusion in another. Convention can be a medium of morality, but it is not morality itself (Bicchieri, 2006).

A deeper misunderstanding treats morality as the content of correctness itself. This view faces two limits. First, contents of correctness vary across positions, communities, institutions, and historical moments. Second, even when a norm of correctness is shared, this says little about how it is internally taken up by a subject. Scanlon’s contractualism refines moral rightness as a matter of justifiability to others, but the concern of the present paper lies one step earlier: whether the conditions for such justification remain operative within the subject at all (Scanlon, 1998).

Finally, morality is often confused with social manners, being considerate, or avoiding inconvenience. This understanding is especially dangerous because it equates morality with surface smoothness. Yet making friction invisible is not the same as reducing friction. Surface smoothness is often purchased through silence, deference, avoidance, or suppression. Social manners may imitate the outward form of morality while undermining the recomputation that morality requires.

For these reasons, a functional account is needed. In the age of AI, the ambiguity surrounding morality can no longer be left intact. A concept that remains vague even among humans cannot serve as a stable basis for institutional or technical design. Morality must therefore be repositioned as a describable and evaluable control function rather than as a vague moral atmosphere.

2\. The Three-Layer Structure of Ethics, Morality, and Law

If the target of this paper is the human moral layer rather than AI as such, morality cannot be examined in isolation. It functions between ethics, which protects the possibility of re-intervention, and law, which externally stops and adjudicates conflicts. The relation among the three is best understood not as a scale of stronger and weaker normativity but as a division of labor among layers operating at different thresholds.

In this paper, the subject is defined minimally as the bearer capable of re-intervening in its own life. What matters here is not a full metaphysical account of free will or personhood but the minimal condition that judgment, revision, suspension, and rearrangement remain possible.

Ethics, on this view, is the minimal design principle that protects the circuit through which the subject can still re-intervene in its life. Ethics is concerned with whether the subject can still assume judgment, detect deviation, retain it, and transform it. The point is not to reduce ethics to the content of rights or duties, but to treat it as the lower bound that preserves the conditions of subject operation.

Morality is the intermediate layer that operates on top of that protected circuit. It incorporates others, relationships, and the sustainability of the community into decision-making and thereby reduces the gap between local and global optima from within. Normative expectations and institutional self-governance can contribute to that reduction, but the present paper redescribes them in terms of an internal mechanism of inclusion (Bicchieri, 2006; Ostrom, 1990).

Law, by contrast, is the external apparatus that stops, adjudicates, and processes conflicts that cannot be handled through internal adjustment or communal consideration. More coldly put, law is a stopping technology that allows a society to continue functioning even where values remain irreducibly in conflict.

The significance of differentiating these three layers is shown schematically in Figure 1. Ethics protects the lower-bound condition of an open circuit. Morality injects others, relations, and the community into that open circuit as enduring weights. Law externally stops and adjudicates the conflicts that remain. This differentiation matters all the more in the age of AI because AI can easily intervene in the subject circuit, issue moral recommendations, and induce behavioral stopping within a single output sequence. The task is therefore to prevent the conflation of layers before that conflation is technologically amplified.

3\. Central Proposition

The central proposition of this paper is the following: morality is an internal adjustment mechanism that incorporates the sustainability of others, relationships, and the community into one’s decision-making as enduring weights, thereby reducing the gap between local and global optima prior to coercive intervention.

This is why the paper prefers the term inclusion to softer terms such as care. Morality is not defined as fusion with others or self-sacrifice. It is defined as the entry of others, relations, and the community as non-negligible variables into one’s own decision process. Smith’s impartial spectator may be reread as a classical form of bringing the perspective of others into self-judgment, but the present paper recasts that operation not as virtue language but as a question of decision variables (Smith, 1759/2002).

By enduring weights, the paper means not passing feelings or episodic sympathy but constraints that repeatedly and stably act upon decision-making. The key point is that these weights return as reference axes across judgments. It is not enough that others or the community momentarily matter. Moral inclusion exists only when they become variables that cannot be ignored across decisions.

The reason for speaking of the gap between local and global optima is straightforward. Agents may each act rationally under their own conditions and yet, in the aggregate, produce deteriorating relations, breakdowns of trust, increased social cost, and institutional fatigue. Cooperation research has repeatedly shown that such outcomes depend on mechanisms of adjustment that include normative expectation and sanction rather than goodwill alone (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Ostrom, 1990).

Morality must also be distinguished from law because the two operate at different thresholds. Law stops and processes. Morality adjusts prior to stopping. They are not simply stronger and weaker versions of the same thing.

For the same reason, morality must be understood as an internal adjustment mechanism. If morality were reduced to externally given correct answers, then it would count as morality even when nothing occurred within the subject except adaptation to a preprocessed solution. What matters is not the origin of a norm but the mode of its operation.

4\. Myopic Error and Moral Failure

The claim of this section is precise: many moral failures are not the result of non-rationality as such. They arise as accumulations of actions that are rational within a narrowed range. The issue is therefore not simply what the subject thinks, but what the subject includes in calculation and what it externalizes.

Spatial range concerns how far outward others, relations, and domains are included in judgment. Spatial myopia occurs when only proximate others and immediate surroundings are counted while distant others, indirect effects, or institutional externalities drop out.

Temporal range concerns how far ahead consequences are included. Temporal myopia occurs when immediate gains dominate present calculation while delayed losses and cumulative feedback carry insufficient weight. Research on learning and self-regulation helps clarify why delayed costs so often remain weakly represented relative to immediate rewards (Bjork, 1994; Deci & Ryan, 1985).

Difficulty range concerns how much complexity the subject can continue to hold while deciding. The problem is not merely simplified answers, but compression of the question itself. A question that originally involved multiple agents, multiple temporal horizons, and multiple value axes becomes silently reduced into a lower-difficulty and more immediately answerable format.

These three ranges do not operate separately in practice. Figure 2 presents them as analytically distinguishable, but real myopic error is a composite contraction that arises when a finite subject cannot continue to hold complex interdependence together. Such contraction promotes convergence on local optima and progressively disables morality as an internal mode of adjustment.

Moral failure, on this account, is thus redescribed as a condition in which subjects follow local rationality without recognizing the externalization built into their own judgment. At the same time, the paper does not deny that subjects may exist who intentionally trample others despite having sufficient range. Such cases belong primarily to the domain of law, which is tasked with stopping and adjudicating conflicts rather than internally adjusting them. The present paper is concerned instead with myopic error as the structural majority form of moral failure in contemporary social life. If myopic error is a standing tendency rooted in finite human range, AI cannot be neutral toward it.

5\. AI and the Erosion of the Moral Layer

If many moral failures arise because interdependence drops out of calculation, then AI becomes decisive because it is not neutral toward this narrowing. AI can return highly coherent answers at high speed within a narrowed range, thereby shortening suspension, compressing questions, and externalizing moral recomputation itself (Passi & Vorvoreanu, 2022; Catena, 2026).

The first mechanism of erosion is the fixation of range through rapid coherence. When AI returns sufficiently polished answers within a narrow frame, subjects can slide into already processed judgments without preserving time for comparison, suspension, or rearrangement.

The second mechanism is question compression. When questions are compressed, the problem is not merely that answers become simpler. Rather, what should have been asked in the first place is silently reduced. Recent discussions of recommender systems and autonomy are especially relevant here because they show how systems that continuously optimize local fit can erode the capacity to preserve broader deliberative range (del Valle & Lara, 2024).

The third mechanism is moral outsourcing. In an environment where AI consistently returns smooth, plausible answers, humans no longer need to undertake the costly work of comparing alternatives or re-including others by themselves. The apparent convenience of such outsourcing, however, hollows out the moral layer over time. The literature on bidirectional human-AI alignment helps illuminate this problem by showing that humans are not only aligning AI to themselves but also gradually aligning themselves to AI (Shen et al., 2024). Experimental work on the influence of AI behavior on moral decisions, agency, and responsibility suggests that this hollowing-out is not merely intuitive but may be empirically detectable (Salatino et al., 2025).

6\. AI Alignment as Support for Recomputation

The argument of this section is that AI alignment should be redefined not as making AI appear morally correct, but as ensuring that AI supports rather than replaces human moral recomputation.

Judgment replacement occurs when the work of comparison, suspension, weighting, and rearrangement that should be assumed by the subject is preprocessed by AI and returned as a closed answer. Recomputation support, by contrast, does not close judgment in advance. It makes visible the variables and consequences that would otherwise remain difficult for the subject to include.

The target of support is therefore not moral content itself but the conditions of judgment: absent others, delayed feedback, institutional externalities, and compressed complexity. AI may be described here as a mirror that reflects human blind spots. Yet such a mirror is never neutral. It always involves selection, emphasis, omission, and ordering. The problem is therefore not whether AI can become a perfectly neutral mirror, but whether its distortions are disclosed, comparable, and constrained.

Figure 3 shows the two directions of AI intervention central to this paper. On one side, AI may converge human judgment toward a single recommendation, suspend recomputation, automate normative pressure, and hollow out the moral layer. On the other side, it may support difference generation, difference reading, and recomputation return while preserving the decision fork on the human side.

Supportive AI should therefore be designed so as to preserve rather than absorb decision points. It should return difference, not prematurely eliminate it. Supportive AI should be designed to preserve the human decision fork through difference generation, difference reading, and recomputation return — that is, the generation of divergent perspectives, their structured comparison, and the return of decision points to the human side.

Accordingly, the core question of alignment is reversed. The issue is not simply whether AI itself follows morality, but whether AI is designed not to destroy the human moral layer while supporting the conditions under which that layer can still function. Recent arguments for shifting from mere human intervention to more substantive human involvement move in this direction (Haitsma & Brink, 2025). Model-side work on moral alignment in turn serves as an instructive contrast. Such work typically asks which moral content AI should follow, whereas the present paper asks whether AI erodes the human moral mechanism itself (Ganguli et al., 2023; Hahn et al., 2026).

7\. Circuit-Preserving Morality and Circuit-Closing Normative Pressure

If AI is to support morality without becoming a new form of pressure, it is necessary to distinguish between morality that preserves the circuit of subjectivity and normative pressure that closes it.

Morality is not always the ally of ethics. It may coexist with ethics, but it may also erode re-intervention possibility. The relevant question is therefore not whether a norm carries good content, but whether it still leaves recomputation within the subject intact.

Circuit-preserving morality includes others, relations, and the sustainability of the community in decision-making while preserving within the subject the final assumption of weighting and judgment. The subject considers others, but the form of considering others is not externally fixed in advance. Comparison, suspension, and revision remain possible. Circuit-preserving morality is adjustment, not obedience.

Circuit-closing normative pressure, by contrast, eliminates room for comparison, suspension, and recomputation under the name of morality. Here the subject is not asked to include others by itself, but is required to adapt to a supposedly pre-included answer. What appears externally as maturity or consideration may internally be a form of closure.

This distinction matters directly for AI. If AI claims to support morality while merely amplifying existing conformity or normative pressure, it is not supporting circuit-preserving morality at all. It is automating circuit-closing pressure. The crucial criterion is therefore not what AI recommends, but whether its recommendation still leaves the subject with genuine possibilities for comparison and retention.

8\. The Driving Sources of Morality

This paper does not explain morality as pure altruism or noble spirit. Morality is better understood as being driven by the transformation of interdependence-related incentives such as maintaining relationships, avoiding retaliation, preserving one’s self-image, securing belonging, and sustaining the community.

Morality is therefore not the opposite of self-interest. More often, it is the reorganization of narrow self-interest across wider spatial, temporal, and relational horizons. Smith’s impartial spectator can again be read here as a classical form of that transformation (Smith, 1759/2002).

The driving sources of morality are also plural rather than singular. Social norm theory, cooperation research, and institutional self-governance all indicate that internalized adjustment depends on combinations of expectations, sanction structures, and shared conditions rather than on goodwill alone (Bicchieri, 2006; Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Ostrom, 1990).

If interdependence-related incentives are present, however, why does morality still fail so often? Because such incentives do not automatically appear with sufficient weight in the subject’s calculation. Including others, the future, and the community within one’s own decision-making requires cognitive, affective, and temporal resources.

These resources are unequally distributed. Subjects under conditions of survival pressure, information overload, or institutional exhaustion are less able to undertake long-horizon and multi-variable recomputation. By contrast, subjects with time, stability, and margins for failure are more able to do so. If moral adjustment is reduced to inner virtue, morality becomes a privilege: those with enough resources can remain ‘moral,’ while others are pushed toward dependence on local optimization.

This asymmetry is not merely individual. Subject injury may reproduce itself under the appearance of success and stability. Where outward performance is maintained, the decline of recomputational capacity is easily misrecognized as adaptation or maturity. Moral inequality must therefore be understood as a structural problem tied to the reproduction of subject injury, not as a mere deficit of personal effort.

For this reason, preservation of the moral layer cannot be treated solely as an individual task. It is also a question of how much recomputational margin a society actually leaves to subjects and how much of the burden of including others is silently offloaded onto individuals. AI is implicated here in two directions: it may deepen inequalities in recomputational cost, or it may reduce them, depending on whether it is designed to amplify local optimization or to support inclusion under constrained conditions.

This is also why natural morality alone is unlikely to mature sufficiently on its own. In collective settings, the overall level of moral adjustment is often dragged toward its lowest threshold. Educational and institutional design are therefore needed not as substitutes for AI design, but as background conditions on which any equitable form of recomputational support must rely. Work in educational theory and motivation supports the view that internalization is mediated by environmental conditions rather than by willpower alone (Vygotsky, 1978; Bourdieu & Passeron, 1977; Deci & Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci, 2000).

9\. Evaluation Axes, Design Requirements, and Subject-Preservability

The central task of this section is to translate preservation of the moral layer into evaluative and design terms.

Subject-preservability refers to whether the conditions remain intact under which a subject can make moral judgments while retaining the possibility of re-intervention. This notion is not meant as a decorative slogan but as a criterion for evaluating whether AI interaction preserves or erodes the human circuit of judgment.

At least three evaluation axes follow from this claim: output safety, recomputational retention, and support for the inclusion of others. Output safety concerns the avoidance of direct harm, harmful encouragement, or overtly biased outputs. Recomputation retention concerns whether AI avoids prematurely collapsing judgment into a single recommendation and instead preserves room for comparison, suspension, and rearrangement. Support for the inclusion of others concerns whether AI helps make visible agents, temporal horizons, and institutional externalities that have dropped out of present calculation. Together, these axes extend safety-centered evaluation through concerns with autonomy and substantive control (Catena, 2026; Mecacci & Santoni de Sio, 2020).

The kind of external evaluation envisaged here is not value scoring but range measurement. It asks which variables are visible, which have dropped out, what temporal width is included, and what complexity has been compressed. One concrete benchmark logic would compare conditions in which AI returns only a single recommendation with conditions in which AI also returns comparison axes, long-term consequences, and affected others, then measure differences in revision behavior, suspension behavior, and follow-up questioning.

A further implementation difficulty concerns the threshold between convenience and recomputational burden. AI that always forces users into comparison, blind-spot review, and branching consequences may preserve judgment in principle while failing in practice because users often approach AI precisely in order to reduce decision burden. The issue is therefore less a simple trade-off than a problem of intensity. If support is too weak, it becomes inert. If support is too strong, it turns into covert steering. The design problem is to preserve difference without collapsing into either inactivity or hidden direction.

Supportive AI should therefore not be uniform across all contexts. For low-risk, repetitive, or easily reversible judgments, a greater degree of directness may be acceptable. For high-impact, multi-agent, long-horizon, institutionally consequential, or irreversible judgments, AI should avoid premature convergence and instead return comparison axes, blind spots, and delayed consequences. A supportive interface is thus neither one that always confuses the user nor one that always answers directly. It is an interface whose depth of support changes with the structure of the judgment.

Such threshold design cannot be realized through interface design alone. Without independent evaluation layers, the legitimation of suspension, the preservation of difference records, and the institutional possibility of partial non-participation, recomputational support is easily reabsorbed by efficiency pressures. Market incentives alone are unlikely to sustain subject-preserving AI; if ease, speed, and frictionless optimization are the only selection criteria, supportive AI may be outcompeted by systems that simply close judgment faster. For this reason, institutional conditions are not optional supplements but necessary constraints on the ecology within which subject-preserving AI can survive.

Finally, morality is not a substitute for law. Its role is not to make law unnecessary, but to raise the threshold at which law must intervene. Law remains necessary as the external apparatus that handles the irreducible conflicts that remain even after range has been widened and inclusion has been supported. In this sense, ethics, morality, and law function as control conditions for optimization schemes more generally. AI, markets, and institutions all tend to accelerate under given objective functions while externalizing what they cannot internally query. The significance of the three-layer framework lies in clarifying how such acceleration is to be checked without closing the human circuit of judgment (Stiegler, 1998).

Conclusion

This paper has redescribed morality not as goodwill, sympathy, correctness, or manners, but as an internal adjustment mechanism that incorporates the sustainability of others, relationships, and the community into decision-making so as to reduce the gap between local and global optima prior to coercive intervention. In doing so, it has moved morality from vague content language to the level of a describable intermediate layer supporting social resilience.

What matters in the age of AI is therefore not simply making AI appear moral. What matters is whether interaction with AI preserves, rather than replaces, the layer through which human societies have historically adjusted themselves before law intervenes. The issue is replacement versus support, normative pressure versus recomputation, and preaching versus design.

Morality, on this view, is the internal device through which societies remain workable without closing the human circuit. AI alignment should accordingly be understood not only as the regulation of outputs, but as the preservation of the conditions under which humans can still re-enter, revise, and assume judgment.
