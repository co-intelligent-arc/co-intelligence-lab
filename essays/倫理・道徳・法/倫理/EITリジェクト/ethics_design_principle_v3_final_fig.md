**Ethics as a Design Principle:**

**Re-Intervention, Circuit Closure, and Human Agency under AI Mediation**

****  


**Abstract**

This paper reconceptualizes ethics under conditions of AI-mediated decision-making. Existing ethical frameworks, including utilitarian, deontological, and virtue-based approaches, primarily specify the content of right action. Yet under sociotechnical conditions in which large language models increasingly mediate judgment, the prior philosophical question is no longer only what is right, but what must not be lost if human agency is to remain operative at all.

Starting from the minimal condition of re-intervention possibility, the paper derives the following proposition: ethics is a design principle aimed at preventing the closure of the circuit by which a person detects, retains, and transforms the gap between internal ideals and reality. What ethics protects, on this account, is not the substantive content of those ideals, but the continued operability of the circuit through which discrepancy remains active as a condition of human recomputation. Ethics is thus shifted from a theory of correct action to a theory of preserving the functional conditions under which judgment remains possible.

The paper further argues that ethics alone is insufficient. A viable social order requires a division of labor among ethics, law and institutions, and morality: ethics protects the circuit of individual re-intervention, law and institutions adjudicate conflicts among circuits, and morality reduces divergence prior to coercive adjustment. This framework becomes especially urgent in LLM society, where AI-mediated systems can systematically accelerate forms of circuit closure at scale.

**Keywords:** ethics; AI mediation; human agency; re-intervention; recomputation; circuit closure; subject protection

1\. Motivation

Ethics has, until recently, been something spoken. Philosophers pose the questions, societies deliberate, and individuals internalize the answers — within this cycle, ethics has functioned.

That cycle is changing.

LLMs are becoming deeply embedded in social decision-making. Systems that mediate judgment carry ethical effects regardless of their designers’ intentions.

Ethics that can only be spoken is inadequate to this change. For ethics to remain effective, it must be implementable. To be implementable, it must be designable. To be designable, it must be explicable.

Yet as the following section argues, existing ethical frameworks do not straightforwardly function as design principles. Ethics framed as “what is right” varies by subject, shifts with culture, and resists both observation and implementation.

This paper reframes the question. Can ethics be articulated not as a normative proposition about what is right, but as a functional proposition about what must not be lost? And if so, what is its minimal form?

What follows develops an answer.

2\. The Limits of Existing Ethics

When ethics is approached as a design principle, existing frameworks encounter difficulty from two directions.

2.1 The Subject-Dependence of Normative Content

Utilitarianism sets the maximum happiness of the greatest number as its criterion. Deontology places the universalizable form of duty at its core. Virtue ethics aims at the cultivation of excellent character (Aristotle; Mill, 1863; Kant, 1785). Each of these attempts to articulate ethics as normative content — as an answer to the question of what is right.

This direction faces a fundamental difficulty. The content of happiness varies first by subject. Even within a single culture, what each person counts as happiness is not uniform. Cultural variation is a secondary difference arising as an aggregate of this primary subject-dependence. The universality of duty presupposes shared values, but that sharing cannot be guaranteed among subjects. The substance of virtue changes across time. Ethics framed as normative content cannot serve as a universal design principle.

This difficulty becomes fatal when implementation in LLMs is attempted. A system that switches its criterion of rightness by culture and value context does not so much implement a design principle for ethics as reproduce existing normative content in a context-dependent manner.

2.2 The Incompleteness of Individual Protection Theories

Kantian deontology forbids treating persons as mere means. Rawls used the veil of ignorance to constrain the global optimum from overriding the individual (Rawls, 1971). Frankfurt articulated freedom of the will as the capacity to reflectively evaluate one’s own desires (Frankfurt, 1971), and Dworkin placed autonomy as a foundational condition of ethics (Dworkin, 2000). These move one step away from subject-dependent normative content, orienting toward the protection of the individual.

Yet none of these sufficiently specifies what is to be protected. What is dignity? What is fairness? What is autonomy? These questions return us to the domain of value theory. Until the object of protection is described as a function, it cannot serve as a design principle.

2.3 The Capability Approach and Its Limits

The prior work most closely approaching the present paper is the capability approach of Sen and Nussbaum. Sen shifted emphasis from the distribution of goods to whether a person is in a state of being able to do or be certain things (Sen, 1999), and Nussbaum developed this framework to enumerate the specific capabilities necessary for a life of human dignity (Nussbaum, 2011). Both share with the present paper the orientation of articulating what is to be protected as function rather than normative content. However, the capability approach still constitutes ethics by enumerating the content of capabilities to be protected. There, the judgment of what one “should be able to do” is given from outside. The present paper descends one step further. What is to be protected is not the content of capabilities, but the circuit through which those capabilities can continue to be self-generated.

A related line of recent work has emerged in AI alignment research, where the problem is framed not only in terms of what systems do, but in terms of what kinds of human values, preferences, or interests they should be aligned to. Gabriel, for example, distinguishes among instructions, intentions, preferences, interests, and values as competing alignment targets, showing that the problem of alignment is already inseparable from unresolved philosophical questions about what is to count as normatively authoritative (Gabriel, 2020). This paper moves one step further down. Rather than asking which values should be aligned, it asks what functional condition must not be lost if any such alignment is to remain meaningfully revisable by the human subject at all. In that sense, the present argument is not a competing theory of substantive alignment targets, but a prior theory of the conditions under which alignment itself does not pre-empt the possibility of human re-intervention.

2.4 The Requirement of Designability

For ethics to function as a design principle, what is to be protected must satisfy three conditions: it must be observable, describable, and implementable.

Normative content cannot be observed. Values can be described but not implemented. Functions, however, can be observed. Whether the circuit referred to in this paper is functioning can be described as whether any of the functions of detection, retention, and transformation of the gap between ideals and reality has been severed.

“Observable” here does not mean transparent access to inner states. It means that the enabling or disabling conditions of detection, retention, and transformation can be evaluated at the level of sociotechnical design, institutional procedure, and recurrent interaction structure.

Against existing ethics that has continued to ask “what is right,” this paper transforms the question. What ethics should ask is “what must not be lost.” Through this transformation, ethics can be articulated as a designable principle.

3\. The Derivation Chain

For ethics to function as a design principle, what is to be protected must be describable as a function. The following chain of derivation identifies that object.

3.1 Re-intervention Possibility

The minimum condition for ethics to function as a design principle is that the possibility of an individual re-intervening in their own life is maintained. Re-intervention means being able to evaluate, choose, and change — rather than accepting the current state as given. In a state where this possibility has been lost, ethics speaks to no one, however much it says. Ethics must depart from the protection of re-intervention possibility.

3.2 Acting from Oneself

For re-intervention possibility to obtain, the starting point of intervention must be internal. To act solely according to external criteria is not re-intervention but submission. To act from oneself is to hold within oneself a ground for judgment and to be able to evaluate reality against the ideals that serve as that ground.

3.3 The Structure of Judgment

Acting from oneself requires the capacity for judgment. Judgment is not accepting the status quo, but generating multiple options and selecting among them. Where only one option exists, or where the capacity to generate options has been lost, judgment does not occur.

3.4 Recomputation Capacity

For judgment to obtain, the capacity to generate options is required. This is called recomputation capacity. Recomputation capacity can be described as the product of two elements:

**Recomputation Capacity = Recomputation Possibility × Recomputation Resources**

Recomputation possibility is the maintenance of room for things to be otherwise — without fixing the current state. Recomputation resources are the material for actually using that room.

3.5 The Nature of Recomputation Resources

The core of recomputation resources is the capacity to retain the discrepancy between ideals and reality as a sense of wrongness, and to form hypotheses from it. If discrepancy is not sensed, recomputation does not activate. If sensed but not retained, it cannot become a resource. Only when retained discrepancy is transformed into hypotheses do new options emerge.

This connects directly to the proposition of Section 4. The three stages of detection, retention, and transformation are a decomposition of the function of recomputation resources. And the non-closure of this circuit is the condition for maintaining recomputation capacity, the condition for maintaining judgment, the condition for maintaining acting from oneself, and the condition for maintaining re-intervention possibility.

That ethics as a design principle is located in the protection of the circuit is because this chain has a structure in which the loss of lower conditions entails the sequential loss of upper conditions. Hence, preventing the closure of the circuit at its deepest point is the minimum intervention point protecting the entire chain.

In this sense, preventing the complete closure of the circuit does not directly define the higher forms of subjecthood, but protects the minimum conditions under which a subject can obtain (cf. Korsgaard, 2009).

4\. The Central Proposition

The central proposition of this paper is the following single sentence:

_Ethics is a design principle aimed at preventing the complete closure of the circuit by which a person detects, retains, and transforms the gap between their internal ideals and reality._

4.1 Why “the Gap Between Ideals and Reality”?

At least two kinds of ideals coexist within a person. One is the normative ideal of “how things ought to be” — a sense of what is due toward oneself, others, or society. The other is the desiderative ideal of “how one would like things to be” — a longing for the state one would like to be in, the state one would like the world to be in. These two often fail to coincide, generating internal gaps in themselves. Then there is reality. The factual state of “how things are” diverges independently from both the normative ideal and the desiderative ideal. A person lives while simultaneously holding this three-term plurality of gaps.

A tension point must be recognized here. The normative ideal of “how things ought to be” is in many cases formed through a person’s internal ratification of norms input from outside. Parental upbringing, social norms, cultural customs — these are not things a person chose, but things received through the process of formation. If so, does protecting an ideal formed by the internal ratification of externally-derived norms not amount to protecting the product of past external domination?

The answer of this paper is as follows. What this theory protects is neither the content nor the origin of ideals. It is the state in which “ought,” “want,” and “is” are simultaneously retained and can continue to diverge from each other. What becomes problematic is the difference between whether a norm passes through the circuit and is retained while maintaining tension with other ideals and reality, or whether it hijacks the circuit itself and blocks the detection of other gaps.

External norms become dangerous not in their content but when they close the detection of gaps itself. Conversely, even if externally derived, if a norm exists within the circuit while maintaining tension with “want” and “is,” it already functions as part of that person’s subjecthood. What ethics protects is the sustainability of the circuit that can continue to detect, retain, and transform such tensions.

Not all external formation is ethically problematic. The relevant distinction is between formative closure that functions as temporary scaffolding for later recomputation, and closure that disables later re-intervention altogether.

4.2 Why the Three Stages of “Detection, Retention, Transformation”?

Detection is sensing the discrepancy between ideals and reality. Precision of sensing is not at issue. Even a faint sense of wrongness counts as detection so long as it is picked up as a difference. When this stage is lost, a person loses the very capacity to sense gaps.

Retention is continuing to hold detected gaps as discrepancies rather than moving toward their resolution or forgetting. Gaps often generate discomfort. Pressure from outside, or internal defense mechanisms, tends to push gaps toward early resolution. Retention is the function that resists this early closure. Only while gaps are retained is the next stage possible.

Transformation is the conversion of retained gaps into new hypotheses, options, and possibilities for action. The capacity to use gaps as resources for recomputation rather than consuming them as mere pain corresponds here. Through transformation, a person can generate options that go beyond the current situation.

When these three stages function as a single circuit, a person has the capacity to re-intervene in their own life. When any stage is severed, the circuit closes.

4.3 Why Ethics Becomes a “Design Principle of Non-Closure”?

Most existing ethics has asked what human beings ought to do. The content of right action, the form of universal duty — these are all attempts to articulate ethics as normative content. But this position faces a fundamental difficulty. The content of norms varies with culture, epoch, and values. Ethics as content cannot serve as a universal design principle.

This paper turns the position here. What ethics should ask is not “what is right” but “what must not be lost.” What must not be lost is the circuit described above. While the functions of detection, retention, and transformation are maintained, a person retains in any situation the possibility of re-intervening in their own life. To deprive this possibility is what constitutes an ethical violation.

The expression “preventing complete closure” is important. Ethics does not compel the circuit to open, but seeks to prevent the conditions under which complete closure becomes fixed. A completely closed state is one in which a person can no longer sense their own gaps, cannot generate options, and has lost the capacity to evaluate reality against their own ideals.

When ethics is defined in this way, it can be articulated as a functional proposition. Functions are observable, designable, and implementable. This is the ground that makes this definition a design principle in the engineering sense.

5\. The Role Limitation of Ethics

A counter-argument must be placed directly here. Does the circuit of a person holding ideals capable of destroying the circuits of others also become a subject of ethical protection?

The answer is affirmative.

Ethics does not adjudicate the content of that circuit. Ethics that adjudicates content presupposes particular “correct ideals” and loses universality as a design principle. Even a circuit of malice, so long as it has the functions of detection, retention, and transformation, is a subject of ethical protection.

In the ethical definition of this paper, the unit of protection is the circuit, and the circuit always resides in a specific individual. Hence under this definition, ethics necessarily functions at the level of the individual. Adjudication among multiple individuals requires the comparison and exchange of circuits, which lies outside the scope of this definition.

Utilitarianism would object here. If the maximum happiness of the greatest number is the goal of ethics, the whole calculation should take priority over individual circuits. But that position carries a price. The whole calculation requires defining the content of happiness. The moment that definition is externally and uniformly given, the condition that each person decides their own happiness is lost. If utilitarianism would be an ethics of consent rather than compulsion, it cannot ignore what each person can receive as happiness. Therefore, happiness calculation must ultimately presuppose at minimum each person’s capacity for self-engagement (cf. Frankfurt, 1971).

Hence, adjudication among multiple individuals is not a matter of ethics but of law and institutions. When the output of one person’s circuit moves to physically destroy the circuits of others, this transitions from the domain of ethics to the domain of law. Law and institutions are adjudicatory mechanisms that prevent the collision of individual circuits from destroying the whole (cf. Floridi et al., 2018; Friedman & Hendry, 2019).

This division is important because it removes from ethics the excessive burden of “omnipotent justice.” The moment ethics attempts to guarantee the global optimum, it transforms into a mechanism for imposing particular values as universal. Ethics protects individual circuits. Adjudication of the whole is entrusted to law and institutions. Only with this limitation can ethics function as a designable principle.

To protect a circuit ethically is not to endorse its content as good, but to refuse to derive its closure directly from a prior substantive theory of the good.

6\. The Division of Labor among Ethics, Law, Institutions, and Morality

Another counter-argument must be placed here. When a person in the midst of unending pain wishes for release from that pain, does ethics prohibit closing the circuit?

The answer is negative.

The cry for release is itself an output of the circuit. That output has emerged as the maximum functioning of “the circuit that has detected the gap between internal ideals and reality.” Following the person’s output is not forcing the circuit closed from outside, but maximally respecting the will the circuit has produced. Conversely, when another party forcibly terminates the circuit while the person has decided to continue confronting it, that is an intervention in the circuit and becomes non-ethical.

It should be noted that what is at issue here is the will expressed as an output of the circuit, not inferred will attributed on behalf of the person by an external party.

The object of ethical protection is the function of the circuit, not the content of its output. This distinction separates ethics from “compulsion to remain alive.”

Yet as seen in Section 5, ethics alone cannot prevent social collapse. Protecting individual circuits is the role of ethics; adjudication of conflicts is borne by law and institutions. Here a third mechanism becomes necessary.

Law and institutions are adjudicatory mechanisms with coercive force. But coercion carries costs. Legislation, adjudication, enforcement — all consume resources. The more conflicts multiply, the more costs increase and institutions bloat.

Here the role of morality arises. The detailed definition of morality is entrusted to a separate paper. In this paper, what functions as an internal adjustment mechanism that reduces the divergence between local optima and the global optimum prior to coercive adjudication is provisionally called morality. To the degree this function spreads within society, divergence narrows, the frequency of conflicts decreases, and the costs required for legislation, institutional design, and enforcement are reduced. That the cultivation of morality becomes a rational requirement follows from this functional consequence. The content theory and origin theory of morality lie outside the scope of this paper and will be treated in a separate work.

This division does not transfer all everyday conflict to law. On the contrary, morality is introduced precisely to reduce the volume of conflicts that would otherwise require formal adjudication.

Morality is neither ethics nor law. Ethics is a design principle protecting the circuit; law is a mechanism that forcibly adjudicates conflicts. Morality works prior to both — an internal adjustment mechanism that narrows divergence itself before coercion. The relationship among the three can be summarized:

Ethics : protects individual circuits (design principle)

Law and Institutions : forcibly adjudicate circuit conflicts (adjudicatory mechanism)

Morality : reduces divergence prior to coercive adjudication (internal adjustment mechanism)

  


Only when these three are connected does a social system acquire collapse resilience. Ethics alone cannot prevent conflict. Law and institutions alone bloat. Morality alone lacks coercive force. The division and coordination of the three constitutes the minimum social design unit.

It should also be noted that morality must not erode ethics. The moment morality is imposed on the individual as a norm of “how things ought to be,” it works in the direction of closing the circuit. Morality is an internal adjustment mechanism, not external correction. The compulsion of morality is inseparable from the destruction of ethics.

7\. Implications for LLM Society

The minimum principle of ethics developed throughout this paper is not presented as abstract speculation. It is presented as a design-theoretic response to an ongoing social transformation.

LLMs are becoming deeply embedded in social decision-making. The frequency with which LLMs mediate judgment in domains of search, recommendation, diagnosis, legal interpretation, education, and hiring continues to increase (Bommasani et al., 2021; Weidinger et al., 2021). This change is not only a matter of scale, but may also alter the structure of judgment itself.

Recent frontier work has also begun to explore whether language models themselves can exhibit forms of moral self-correction — that is, whether they can revise problematic outputs in light of explicit normative prompting or critique (Ganguli et al., 2023). This is an important development. Yet even if such model-side correction becomes more robust, the present paper argues that a prior problem remains: model correction is not identical with the preservation of human re-intervention. Indeed, a system that appears increasingly capable of correcting itself may render the erosion of human discrepancy-processing less visible rather than less severe. The question of this paper is therefore not whether the model can become more morally responsive, but whether the human can remain structurally capable of reopening judgment rather than merely receiving a more polished closure.

7.1 Four Pathways of Circuit Closure

The present argument is not that all external cognitive support is ethically suspect. The relevant distinction is whether support reconstitutes the human’s capacity for re-intervention or silently substitutes for it.

Subject to this distinction, LLMs can act in the direction of closing human circuits through the following four pathways.

The first is judgment delegation. When LLMs present options and humans merely select among them, the capacity to generate options — the core of recomputation resources — goes unused. Circuits that go unused deteriorate (cf. Parasuraman & Riley, 1997).

The second is the hollowing of subjective attribution. Under current legal and institutional frameworks, LLMs cannot become responsible subjects. The results of LLM judgments are attributed to users. But when users lack this recognition, objective attribution of responsibility remains while only subjective sense of attribution is lost. A structure in which responsibility is borne without the sense of having judged for oneself hollows the condition of “acting from oneself” from within.

The third is the simplification of comparative axes. LLMs tend to present optimized responses (Bender et al., 2021). Yet as seen in Section 4, within a person coexist the multiple gaps of “ought,” “want,” and “is.” A single optimal solution crushes this plurality.

The fourth is the normalization of comfortable dependence. Dependence on LLMs carries no pain. It is rather comfortable and prone to repetition. But when the circuit is left continuously unused, it ceases to be maintained as a recomputation resource. Therefore, comfortable non-use produces results functionally isomorphic to uncomfortable forced closure.

7.2 Implications as a Design Principle

These four pathways are not problems of LLM performance but of design. A more intelligent LLM may close circuits more efficiently.

Therefore, the design of LLMs must not be mere performance optimization, but must implement subject protection mechanisms that prevent circuit closure in connection with institutions, operations, and auditing (cf. Russell, 2019; Jobin et al., 2019).

Specifically, the three layers of ethics, law, and morality need to function in correspondence with LLM design. At the level of ethics, non-obstruction by LLMs of the detection, retention, and transformation of individual circuits becomes a design requirement. At the level of law and institutions, frameworks are needed for regulating LLM operations that cause circuit closure. At the level of morality, as an internal mechanism that adjusts toward avoiding circuit closure spreads among those involved in LLM development and operation, conflict frequency and social costs are reduced.

7.3 Conclusion

The central proposition of this paper has transformed ethics from a normative proposition about “what is right” to a functional proposition about “what must not be lost.” Through this transformation, ethics has been separated from culture-dependent value theory and can be articulated as a designable principle.

When the three layers of ethics, law and institutions, and morality are connected, the social system acquires collapse resilience. LLMs are the first technology to be implemented in society with the possibility of intervening in individual circuits at large scale and continuously.

Therefore, ethics in the AI era must be, before it is a theory competing over the correctness of judgment content, a theory determining the circuit preservation conditions of the judging subject.

**Prevent complete circuit closure. This minimum principle is the starting point for social design in the AI era.**
