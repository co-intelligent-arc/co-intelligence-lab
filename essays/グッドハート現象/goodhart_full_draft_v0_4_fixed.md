**When Proxies Become Purposes: Reconnection Failure and Goodhartization in Proxy-Based Institutions**

  


**Abstract**

Modern institutions depend on proxies: scores, metrics, rankings, targets, and procedures that make complex purposes tractable under finite cognitive, organizational, and political capacity. This paper proposes the concept of Goodhartization: the process by which proxies introduced to serve complex purposes become stabilized as effective ends in their own right. The difference from familiar accounts of Goodhart's law and Campbell's law lies not only in what goes wrong with the proxy, but in what prevents the institution from noticing and correcting it. This paper argues that Goodhartization should be understood through reconnection failure: the weakening of the circuit through which proxy inadequacy is recognized, articulated, taken up institutionally, and made revisable. The paper further identifies reconnection-cost avoidance as the mechanism by which such failure becomes durable: institutions repeatedly avoid the work of testing, correcting, and transitioning away from proxies because revision is costly, unevenly distributed, and locally rational to defer. In AI-mediated institutions, this dynamic may intensify as AI systems optimize existing proxies more efficiently, though deliberate design may also reduce some costs of reconnection. This framework locates where and why institutional reconnection fails, with implications for the design of proxy-based institutions and for the use of AI as a tool of reconnection rather than optimization alone. The problem is not that institutions use proxies. The problem is that they can lose the capacity to revise them.

  


**Keywords:** Goodhart's law; proxy optimization; reconnection failure; institutional theory; organizational learning; AI-mediated governance

  


**1\. Introduction**

Modern institutions depend on proxies. Educational systems use test scores to represent learning, research systems use publications and citations to represent scholarly contribution, organizations use performance indicators to represent effectiveness, and public institutions use targets and compliance procedures to represent accountability. Such proxies are not merely distortions imposed from outside. They are often introduced because the purposes they stand for are too complex, diffuse, or contested to be handled directly in everyday decision-making. Proxies make complex purposes tractable under finite cognitive, organizational, and political capacity.

The problem, therefore, is not that institutions use proxies. A score, ranking, target, or procedure can help coordinate action, allocate resources, and justify decisions. The problem begins when a proxy ceases to function as partial evidence of a wider purpose and becomes the effective object of optimization. A test score may cease to serve as an imperfect indicator of learning and become the object of educational activity. A citation count may cease to function as a partial sign of scholarly contribution and become a target in its own right. A compliance procedure may cease to support accountability and instead become the visible substitute for accountability itself.

This paper refers to this process as Goodhartization: the process by which proxies, metrics, or procedures introduced to make complex purposes tractable come to operate as ends in their own right. The concept is related to, but broader than, familiar formulations of Goodhart's law and Campbell's law. It does not concern only the corruption of indicators under pressure, nor only the optimization of a faulty metric. It concerns the institutional process through which a proxy becomes stabilized as the effective object of action, evaluation, and justification. The difference lies not in what goes wrong with the proxy, but in what prevents the institution from noticing and correcting it.

The central claim of this paper is that Goodhartization should be understood as a failure of reconnection. A proxy becomes dangerous not simply because it is imperfect. All proxies are imperfect. It becomes dangerous when the circuit that periodically reconnects the proxy to the purpose, or purposes, by which it was introduced or justified begins to weaken. This circuit includes the recognition of divergence, the articulation of that divergence, its uptake within institutional processes, and the possibility of corrective revision. Goodhartization occurs when this circuit no longer functions effectively. This claim is developed not as a predictive model or normative critique alone, but as a conceptual framework for locating where institutional reconnection fails.

This framing avoids two simplifications. First, it does not assume that institutions possess a single true purpose that can be identified from outside. Institutional purposes are often multiple, historically layered, politically contested, and rhetorically stabilized. The question is not whether an external observer can determine the one authentic purpose of an institution, but whether the relation between a proxy and the purpose or normative claim by which it is introduced, maintained, or justified remains examinable and revisable.

Second, the paper does not treat proxy failure as a merely cognitive error. Actors may recognize that a proxy no longer serves the purpose it was meant to support. Teachers may know that a score does not capture learning; researchers may know that publication counts do not capture intellectual contribution; administrators may know that compliance does not exhaust accountability. Yet recognition alone does not produce correction. Reconnection is costly. It requires articulation, institutional uptake, possible redesign of criteria, reallocation of responsibility, revision of incentives, and transition away from established routines. Institutions may therefore continue to rely on a proxy not because it remains adequate, but because examining and correcting its inadequacy would impose cognitive, operational, political, and institutional costs.

The paper develops this argument as a conceptual framework rather than an empirically calibrated model. Its aim is not to provide a predictive formula for when Goodhartization will occur, but to clarify the relations among proxy dependence, evaluative pressure, reconnection-cost avoidance, and reconnection failure. The formal elements introduced later should therefore be read as an operational schema: a way of fixing the direction of conceptual relations, not as a fully specified measurement model.

This paper does not survey the relevant literatures, but reframes them around a single analytical question: when a proxy is used to stand in for a complex purpose, at what point, and under what cost pressures, does the circuit that re-examines its adequacy stop functioning? Existing work explains why indicators become targets, why measures replace constructs, why formal structures become decoupled from practice, why organizations struggle to revise their governing assumptions, and why proxy optimization can fail. The present paper brings these accounts into a common operational frame by focusing on the weakening of the circuit through which proxies are reconnected to the purposes by which they were introduced or justified.

The argument proceeds as follows. Section 2 situates the framework in relation to work on Goodhart's law, Campbell's law, surrogation, metric fixation, institutional decoupling, double-loop learning, and proxy optimization. Section 3 defines Goodhartization and distinguishes it from related forms of indicator failure. Section 4 develops reconnection failure as the proposed mechanism and introduces the minimal operational schema. Section 5 examines reconnection-cost avoidance and the institutional conditions under which proxy dependence becomes self-sustaining. The concluding section considers implications for AI-mediated institutions, where proxy optimization may be intensified, but where reconnection costs may also be deliberately reduced.

  


**2\. Existing Accounts of Proxy Failure and Institutional Reconnection**

Goodhartization does not begin from a theoretical vacuum. A wide range of literatures has examined how indicators become targets, how measures replace what they were meant to represent, how organizations fail to revise governing assumptions, how formal structures become detached from practice, and how proxy optimization can fail. The aim of this section is not to survey these literatures exhaustively. Rather, it identifies what each literature explains and clarifies the analytical gap that motivates the present framework: the need to understand where, and under what cost pressures, the circuit that reconnects proxies to their justifying purposes stops functioning.

**2.1 Goodhart's law, Campbell's law, and indicator failure**

Goodhart's law and Campbell's law provide the most direct starting points for the present argument. Goodhart's original formulation emerged from monetary policy, where statistical regularities used for control may cease to hold once they become targets of policy (Goodhart, 1975). Later formulations generalized this insight into the now familiar claim that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure (Strathern, 1997). Campbell's law makes a parallel point in the context of social indicators: the more a quantitative indicator is used for social decision-making, the more it becomes subject to corruption pressures and the more it may distort the processes it is intended to monitor (Campbell, 1979).

These accounts explain why indicators fail under pressure. They show that measurement is not neutral once it is embedded in systems of evaluation, reward, and control. A proxy that initially helps represent a complex purpose can begin to reshape the behavior it was meant to measure. In this sense, Goodhart's law and Campbell's law identify a central danger of proxy-based governance: indicators do not merely observe institutions; they can reorganize them.

The present paper accepts this insight but shifts the analytical focus. The central question here is not only why indicators become corrupted or cease to measure what they were meant to measure. It is why institutions continue to rely on them once divergence becomes visible or suspected. Indicator failure becomes Goodhartization when the proxy remains institutionally operative despite evidence, experience, or suspicion that its relation to the original purpose has weakened. The relevant question is therefore not only what goes wrong with the proxy, but what prevents the institution from noticing, articulating, taking up, and correcting that failure.

**2.2 Surrogation and metric fixation**

A second relevant literature examines how measures come to replace the constructs, strategies, or purposes they were designed to represent. Research on surrogation shows that decision-makers may treat a measure as if it were the underlying construct or strategy itself (Choi et al., 2012). A performance metric intended to represent customer satisfaction, organizational strategy, or learning may become the object toward which attention and action are directed. Closely related discussions of metric fixation describe how organizations can become excessively attached to quantified indicators, even when those indicators provide only partial or distorted representations of the relevant purpose.

This literature is important because it moves beyond the claim that indicators are externally corrupted. It shows how proxy dependence can become internalized within cognition and practice: once a measure becomes familiar, comparable, reportable, and institutionally rewarded, it may no longer appear as a provisional representation but as the practical substitute for the thing represented.

The present paper treats surrogation and metric fixation as key components of Goodhartization, but not as the whole process. Surrogation explains how a measure replaces a construct in judgment. Metric fixation explains how quantified indicators acquire practical dominance. What remains to be clarified is how such replacement persists when actors recognize its limits. In proxy-based institutions, the problem is not always that actors believe the metric is perfect. It is often that the metric remains cheaper, safer, and more institutionally available than the work required to reconnect it to the purpose it was meant to serve.

**2.3 Double-loop learning and organizational reflexivity**

The argument also stands in relation to work on organizational learning, especially the distinction between single-loop and double-loop learning (Argyris & Schön, 1978). Single-loop learning corrects errors within existing goals, rules, and assumptions. Double-loop learning questions and revises the governing assumptions themselves. This distinction is crucial because Goodhartization is not merely a failure to adjust behavior within an existing evaluative frame. It is a failure to question whether the evaluative frame still serves the purpose for which it was introduced.

For this reason, the present framework should not be read as a replacement for double-loop learning theory. Rather, it builds on the insight that organizations must sometimes revise the assumptions that guide action. The difference lies in the specific problem domain. Double-loop learning explains how organizations can question governing assumptions. This paper asks why, in proxy-based settings, the circuit for such questioning fails to activate, stalls, or becomes institutionally ineffective even when discrepancy is recognized.

This distinction is important. In Goodhartized settings, actors may already know that the proxy is inadequate. Teachers may know that a score does not capture learning; managers may know that a target distorts work; researchers may know that publication counts do not capture intellectual contribution. The obstacle is not simply cognitive blindness. It is that recognition must pass through further institutional stages: articulation, uptake, revision, and transition. Each stage carries costs. Reconnection failure names the weakening of this circuit under the specific conditions of proxy dependence. Reconnection failure is not simply double-loop learning that fails. It names the specific conditions under which the recognition of divergence does not produce institutional revision, because each stage of the circuit carries costs that institutional actors find rational to avoid.

**2.4 Institutional legitimacy, decoupling, and formal structures**

Earlier work on bureaucracy and goal displacement had already noted that organizations may come to prioritize procedural compliance over substantive purpose (Merton, 1940). Institutional theory provides another necessary part of the background. Organizations do not adopt formal structures only because they are technically efficient; they also adopt them because they confer legitimacy, satisfy external expectations, and make organizational action recognizable as proper, rational, or accountable (Meyer & Rowan, 1977). Work on institutional isomorphism further explains how organizations become similar under coercive, mimetic, and normative pressures (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983).

This literature helps explain why proxies and procedures can remain stable even after their practical adequacy becomes questionable. A metric, target, or procedure may be maintained not because it reliably serves the purpose by which it was introduced, but because it has become part of the organization's legitimacy structure. Changing it may require not only technical revision but also explanation, negotiation, responsibility redistribution, and possible acknowledgement that previous decisions were inadequate.

The present paper reframes these insights through the lens of reconnection. Institutional theory explains why formal structures persist and why organizations may decouple appearance from practice. The present framework asks how such persistence affects the circuit through which proxies remain examinable and revisable. When maintaining the proxy is less costly than reopening the purpose it was meant to serve, cost-rational continuity can displace purpose-rational reconsideration. This is the point at which formal stability becomes a mechanism of Goodhartization.

**2.5 Proxy optimization and AI alignment**

A final adjacent literature comes from machine learning and AI alignment, where Goodhart effects are discussed in terms of proxy optimization and classified into multiple variants (Manheim & Garrabrant, 2019). In these settings, systems optimize a specified reward, metric, or objective function, while the intended human purpose remains only imperfectly captured by that proxy. The more powerful the optimizer, the more serious the divergence can become. This literature clarifies why proxy failure becomes especially consequential when optimization capacity increases.

The present paper does not treat AI alignment as a separate topic to be added onto institutional theory. Instead, it treats AI-mediated systems as a contemporary setting in which the dynamics of proxy dependence become intensified. AI systems can accelerate the optimization of existing proxies, producing reports, rankings, recommendations, classifications, and decisions that make the proxy more efficient and more pervasive. In this respect, AI can strengthen Goodhartization by increasing the speed and scale of proxy optimization.

At the same time, AI systems may also reduce some of the costs of reconnection. They can help articulate discrepancies, compare alternative criteria, summarize dispersed feedback, generate revision proposals, and make institutional reconsideration easier to initiate. This possibility does not follow automatically from the presence of AI. The default trajectory is proxy optimization; reconnection-cost reduction requires deliberate design. For the purposes of this paper, AI therefore illustrates the contemporary stakes of the framework rather than replacing its institutional focus.

**2.6 Reframing these literatures around reconnection failure**

Taken together, these literatures explain many of the conditions under which proxies, indicators, metrics, and formal structures become detached from the purposes they were meant to serve. Goodhart's law and Campbell's law explain how indicators fail under pressure. Surrogation and metric fixation explain how measures replace constructs or strategies. Double-loop learning explains the importance of revising governing assumptions. Institutional theory and decoupling explain why formal structures may persist apart from substantive effectiveness. AI alignment and proxy optimization explain how optimization can amplify proxy-purpose divergence.

This paper does not claim that these literatures fail to explain proxy failure. Its claim is more specific. Across these accounts, the relation between proxy failure and institutional reconnection remains analytically dispersed. Existing work explains why indicators become targets, why measures replace constructs, why formal structures persist, why organizations struggle to revise assumptions, and why proxy optimization fails. The present framework brings these insights into a common operational frame by asking: when a proxy is used to stand in for a complex purpose, at what point, and under what cost pressures, does the circuit that re-examines its adequacy stop functioning?

This question leads to the concept of reconnection failure. Reconnection failure refers to the weakening of the circuit through which a proxy remains examinable and revisable in relation to the purpose or normative claim by which it was introduced, maintained, or justified. It is not reducible to ignorance, metric corruption, organizational inertia, or optimization error alone. It concerns the institutional pathway through which divergence must be recognized, articulated, taken up, and made correctable. Goodhartization occurs when this pathway weakens and the proxy becomes stabilized as an effective object of action, evaluation, and justification.

  


**3\. Goodhartization: Recognized Inadequacy Without Revision**

Goodhartization is not primarily a story about bad actors or simple cognitive failure. It is a story about how locally rational behavior under institutional pressure can produce outcomes that no one explicitly chose and that many participants recognize as inadequate. A proxy may persist not because it remains adequate, but because the work of replacing it is costly, unevenly distributed, and institutionally avoidable.

Consider how widely this pattern is recognized. Teachers know that standardized test scores do not capture learning; they shape instruction around them nonetheless. Researchers know that publication counts and journal rankings do not capture intellectual contribution; they optimize for them regardless. Administrators know that compliance procedures do not exhaust accountability; they maintain them because the alternative is harder to justify. Managers know that quarterly targets distort long-term investment; they meet them because the institutional consequences of missing them are immediate and the consequences of distortion are deferred. In each case, the inadequacy is known. What is missing is not recognition but revision.

This is the central paradox that the present paper seeks to explain. Goodhartization does not require that actors be deceived. It requires only that the costs of correcting a proxy fall unevenly, fall on actors without sufficient authority to revise it, or fall in ways that make continued reliance the locally rational choice. The proxy persists not despite being recognized as inadequate, but alongside that recognition.

**3.1 Definition**

Goodhartization is the process by which a proxy, metric, or procedure introduced to make a complex purpose tractable becomes stabilized as an effective end in its own right.

Three features characterize this process.

The first is compression. Complex purposes, such as learning, contribution, accountability, or effectiveness, cannot be handled directly in everyday institutional decision-making. Proxies are introduced as working compressions: partial, imperfect, but useful representations that allow institutions to coordinate action, allocate resources, and justify decisions under finite capacity.

The second is stabilization. Once a proxy is embedded in evaluation, reward, reporting, and legitimacy structures, it acquires institutional weight independent of its adequacy. Changing it requires not only technical revision but explanation, negotiation, redistribution of responsibility, and acknowledgement that prior decisions may have been inadequate. The proxy becomes costly to replace.

The third feature is the most consequential: cost-rational persistence. When the costs of revision fall on those who recognize the problem rather than those who created it, and when the visible costs of continuation are lower than the deferred costs of persisting, institutions do not merely tolerate inadequacy; inadequacy becomes structurally rational to maintain. The actors who recognize the divergence may lack the authority, incentives, or institutional protection required to revise it, while those with the authority to revise it may face costs that make continuation appear safer than correction. This is not dysfunction in the exceptional sense. It is the ordinary operation of institutions under proxy dependence.

**3.2 Distinction from related concepts**

Goodhart's law and Campbell's law explain why indicators fail under pressure. Surrogation and metric fixation explain how measures replace constructs. Decoupling explains how formal structures persist apart from practice. Double-loop learning explains how governing assumptions may be questioned and revised.

These accounts explain important parts of the terrain. Goodhartization names the process by which recognized proxy inadequacy persists because the pathway from recognition to revision is itself institutionally costly, unevenly distributed, and rationally avoidable. Its focus is therefore not only on the failure of measurement, the substitution of a proxy for a construct, or the persistence of formal structures. It is on the institutional conditions under which actors can recognize divergence while the system continues to act as if the proxy remained adequate.

**3.3 Phenomenon and mechanism**

Goodhartization is the phenomenon. Reconnection failure is the proposed mechanism. The following section develops reconnection failure as a circuit with identifiable stages, each carrying costs that can prevent the circuit from completing. The formal schema introduced there is not a predictive model but an operational map of where and why the circuit weakens.

  


**4\. Reconnection Failure: The Circuit from Recognition to Revision**

Section 3 defined Goodhartization as the stabilization of a proxy, metric, or procedure as an effective end in its own right. The present section develops the proposed mechanism: reconnection failure. Reconnection failure refers to the weakening of the circuit through which a proxy remains examinable and revisable in relation to the purpose or normative claim by which it was introduced, maintained, or justified.

The key point is that recognition is only the first step. An actor may recognize that a proxy is inadequate, yet the institution may continue to act as if the proxy were adequate. For correction to occur, recognition must move through a sequence of further stages. It must be articulated, taken up by an institutional process, and translated into revisable criteria, routines, incentives, or responsibilities. Reconnection failure occurs when this sequence weakens or breaks.

**4.1 The reconnection circuit**

The reconnection circuit can be described in four stages: recognition, articulation, uptake, and revision.

Recognition is the perception or suspicion that a proxy no longer adequately serves the purpose it was meant to support. This may arise through experience, feedback, anomalies, unintended consequences, or contradiction between the proxy and observed outcomes. Recognition does not require a fully developed critique. It may begin as unease, practical knowledge, or localized awareness that the proxy is distorting the activity it was meant to guide.

Articulation is the conversion of this recognition into a form that can be communicated, justified, and contested. A teacher may sense that test preparation is displacing learning, but the concern must be expressed in a language that can travel beyond the classroom. A researcher may know that publication counts distort intellectual priorities, but the concern must be stated in terms recognizable to committees, institutions, or disciplines. Without articulation, recognition remains local and institutionally inert.

Uptake is the moment at which articulated concern enters an institutional process. A concern may be voiced but not taken up. It may be treated as anecdotal, inconvenient, insufficiently measurable, politically risky, or outside the mandate of the relevant decision-making body. Uptake requires a channel, a responsible audience, and some recognized authority to treat the concern as institutionally actionable.

Revision is the capacity to alter the proxy, the criteria attached to it, the incentives surrounding it, or the routines through which it is used. Revision may require new indicators, altered evaluation rules, redistributed responsibility, changed reporting practices, or temporary instability during transition. This is the stage at which the relation between proxy and purpose becomes practically revisable rather than merely discussable.

These four stages should not be read as a rigid sequence. In practice, they may overlap, repeat, or occur in partial forms. Their value is analytical: they distinguish between merely noticing a divergence and possessing an institutional pathway through which that divergence can be corrected. Goodhartization becomes durable when this pathway fails to complete.

**4.2 Why recognition is not enough**

The distinction between recognition and revision is central. Many institutional failures are not sustained by ignorance. They are sustained by the inability or unwillingness to convert recognition into institutional change.

A proxy may be widely recognized as inadequate while remaining practically dominant. This can occur when recognition is individualized, when articulation is risky, when uptake is blocked, or when revision would impose costs on powerful actors. In such cases, awareness and action become decoupled. The institution may contain many actors who know that the proxy is distorting the purpose, while the institution as a whole continues to reproduce the proxy.

This is why Goodhartization cannot be reduced to cognitive error. If the problem were simply that actors mistook the proxy for the purpose, then correction would follow from better information. But in many cases, better information is already available. The problem is that information does not automatically become revision. Recognition must pass through institutional structures, and those structures may filter, delay, dilute, or neutralize it.

The circuit therefore matters because it identifies where recognition can fail to become correction. A proxy may survive because the divergence is not perceived. But it may also survive because the divergence cannot be safely articulated, because articulated concerns receive no uptake, or because uptake does not produce revisable criteria. Reconnection failure names this broader institutional condition.

**4.3 The costs of reconnection**

Each stage of the reconnection circuit carries costs.

Recognition carries informational and attentional costs. To recognize divergence, actors must compare the proxy against outcomes, purposes, or normative claims that may be diffuse, contested, or difficult to observe. If the proxy is simple, visible, and institutionally rewarded, while the purpose is complex and distributed, recognition itself becomes effortful.

Articulation carries social and political costs. Naming a divergence can imply that current practices are inadequate, that previous decisions were mistaken, or that powerful evaluative systems are misaligned. Actors may avoid articulation because it invites conflict, exposes responsibility, threatens reputation, or requires more evidence than they can easily provide.

Uptake carries organizational costs. Institutions must decide whether a concern deserves attention, who has authority to address it, and whether it belongs within existing procedures. Concerns that challenge the proxy may be framed as exceptional, subjective, disruptive, or insufficiently actionable. The institution may therefore absorb the signal without changing the structure.

Revision carries the heaviest costs. It may require redesigning metrics, altering incentives, changing reporting routines, redistributing responsibility, renegotiating legitimacy, and accepting transitional uncertainty. Revision is especially costly when the proxy has become embedded in resource allocation, promotion, compliance, reputation, or public accountability.

These costs are not distributed evenly. The asymmetry between those who recognize divergence and those with authority to revise it is examined in the following section.

**4.4 Minimal operational schema**

Two pressures shape this circuit. Compression pressure (ρ) arises when a complex purpose must be simplified into a proxy to enable decision-making. Proxy dependence (P) arises when the proxy becomes embedded in evaluation, reward, routine, and legitimacy structures. Figure 1 above illustrates how the circuit operates as a process; the formal expression below fixes the directional relation among its variables. Both are schematic: ρ and P function as dimensionless conceptual indices rather than measured quantities.

The following formal expression is not offered as an empirically calibrated predictive model. It is a minimal operational schema for representing the direction of the relation between compression pressure, proxy dependence, and reconnection activity:

R = R₀ / (1 + αρ + μP)

Here, R denotes the activity of the reconnection circuit: the degree to which recognition, articulation, uptake, and revision remain operative. R₀ denotes baseline reconnection capacity under low pressure. ρ denotes compression pressure: the pressure to reduce a complex, diffuse, or contested purpose into a manageable proxy. P denotes proxy dependence: the degree to which action, evaluation, reward, legitimacy, or routine has become organized around the proxy. α and μ denote the relative strength with which compression pressure and proxy dependence reduce reconnection activity.

The expression states a limited claim. As compression pressure increases, and as proxy dependence increases, the activity of the reconnection circuit tends to decline. The claim is not that every institution follows this exact function. The claim is that Goodhartization becomes more likely when the very conditions that make proxies useful also reduce the institution's capacity to examine and revise them.

The schema also clarifies why recognition alone is insufficient. A system may contain local recognition while the overall reconnection activity remains low. If articulation is suppressed, uptake is blocked, or revision is too costly, then the effective value of R remains low even when some actors know that the proxy is inadequate. Reconnection failure is therefore not the absence of awareness, but the weakening of the pathway from awareness to correction. In this sense, the expression should be read as a heuristic diagram in symbolic form, not as a mathematical law. In AI-mediated settings, where proxy optimization may be automated and accelerated, the relation between ρ, P, and R may become especially consequential, though the schema remains agnostic about specific institutional contexts.

**4.5 Scope and limitations**

This model is deliberately minimal. It does not claim that compression pressure and proxy dependence are the only factors that reduce reconnection activity. Power relations, institutional history, legal constraints, organizational culture, resource scarcity, and strategic incentives may all shape whether reconnection occurs. The schema does not replace these explanations. It offers a way to locate them within a shared circuit.

Nor does the schema assume that ρ and P can be measured in the same way across individuals, organizations, and institutions. In a classroom, compression pressure may arise from curriculum demands, exam systems, and limited instructional time. In research evaluation, it may arise from the need to compare heterogeneous contributions. In public administration, it may arise from the demand to report complex outcomes through standardized indicators. The relevant measures will differ by context.

Finally, the schema does not treat reconnection as always desirable at every moment. Institutions cannot revise every proxy continuously. Some stabilization is necessary for coordination, accountability, and action. The problem is not stabilization itself, but stabilization without a functioning circuit of reconsideration. Goodhartization occurs when proxies remain stable not because they have been revalidated, but because the costs of reopening them are institutionally avoided.

The next section examines this cost structure more directly. It focuses on reconnection-cost avoidance: the process by which institutions maintain proxy dependence because the work of testing, correcting, and transitioning away from a proxy is more costly than continuing to act as if it remained adequate.

  


**5\. Reconnection-Cost Avoidance: How Proxy Dependence Becomes Self-Sustaining**

The previous section described reconnection failure as a circuit from recognition to revision. This section examines why the circuit does not merely fail once, but becomes repeatedly weakened. The central mechanism is reconnection-cost avoidance: the process by which institutions continue relying on a proxy because examining, correcting, redesigning, or transitioning away from it would impose costs that actors find rational to avoid. Throughout this section, 'institution' refers not to a unified agent but to a distributed configuration of actors, roles, rules, and incentives whose collective effect is to maintain or avoid revising the proxy.

Reconnection-cost avoidance is not simply inaction. It is a patterned institutional response to the costs of reopening the relation between proxy and purpose. Once a proxy has become embedded in evaluation, reward, reporting, legitimacy, or resource allocation, correcting it is no longer a technical adjustment. It becomes a challenge to established routines, previous decisions, authority relations, and distributions of responsibility. The institution may therefore preserve the proxy not because it remains adequate, but because revising it would require work that is costly, conflictual, and unevenly distributed.

**5.1 Avoidance as a repeated institutional pattern**

A single decision to avoid revision may be understandable as local cost management. Institutions cannot revise every metric, procedure, or evaluative frame every time a concern appears. The deeper problem emerges when avoidance becomes repeatable. Each time a concern is not articulated, not taken up, or not converted into revision, the institution learns that the proxy can survive without being reconnected to its purpose.

This produces a cumulative effect. The first avoidance may appear pragmatic. The second becomes easier because a precedent exists. The third can be justified by reference to continuity: the institution has already handled similar concerns without revising the proxy. Over time, the absence of revision becomes evidence for the proxy's stability. What was once a provisional avoidance becomes a routine of institutional self-protection.

In this way, Goodhartization becomes self-sustaining. The proxy remains in place not only because it is useful, but because previous failures to revise it reduce the perceived necessity and legitimacy of future revision. The institution does not merely avoid the cost of reconnection; it learns how to avoid it.

**5.2 Precedent, normalization, and the lowering of avoidance costs**

Reconnection-cost avoidance becomes powerful because it lowers its own future cost. Once an institution has declined to revise a proxy despite recognized inadequacy, several things change.

First, the burden of proof shifts. The proxy no longer has to justify itself each time. Instead, critics must justify why revision is necessary now, despite prior continuity. The longer the proxy remains in place, the more normal it appears.

Second, the vocabulary of concern becomes easier to neutralize. Repeated objections can be treated as familiar complaints, local dissatisfaction, resistance to accountability, or insufficiently actionable feedback. What once might have triggered inquiry can be absorbed into existing categories of noise.

Third, institutional memory of non-revision accumulates as a resource for justification, while familiarity with alternative arrangements diminishes. Actors can appeal to precedent, administrative continuity, or procedural stability, while the institution loses practical familiarity with other ways of representing the purpose. “This is how it has been done” becomes not merely a description, but a reason to continue.

The result is a feedback process: avoidance stabilizes the proxy, and stabilization makes further avoidance easier. This is why Goodhartization cannot be understood only as the moment when a metric becomes distorted. It must also be understood as the institutional learning process through which non-revision becomes easier to repeat. This cycle is not automatically self-closing. It can be interrupted by external shocks, protected channels for dissent, legal mandates, leadership intervention, or institutional routines that require periodic revalidation of proxies. However, such interruptions are not guaranteed by the presence of AI or information alone; they require deliberate institutional design.

**5.3 The asymmetry of costs and authority**

Reconnection-cost avoidance is reinforced by a structural asymmetry between those who recognize divergence and those who can authorize revision. In many institutions, the people closest to the divergence are not the people empowered to change the proxy. Teachers, researchers, managers, and frontline administrators may experience proxy distortions directly, while assessment systems, promotion criteria, funding rules, or compliance procedures are controlled elsewhere.

This asymmetry matters because recognition and authority are separated. Those who recognize the divergence may bear the burden of articulation without possessing the capacity to revise. Those who possess revision authority may be insulated from the practical distortions created by the proxy. They may also face reputational, political, or operational costs if they reopen the proxy's adequacy.

As a result, the institution can contain recognition without producing correction. Knowledge of inadequacy circulates locally, while the authority to revise remains elsewhere. Reconnection-cost avoidance is therefore not only a matter of psychological reluctance. It is a structural condition in which the costs and capacities required for reconnection are unevenly distributed.

**5.4 Local rationality and purpose-level irrationality**

The persistence of a proxy can be locally rational even when it is purpose-level irrational. For a teacher, optimizing for test scores may be locally rational if evaluation systems reward score improvement. For a researcher, optimizing publication counts may be locally rational if hiring and promotion depend on them. For an administrator, maintaining a compliance procedure may be locally rational if it provides visible accountability and reduces exposure to criticism.

These choices may not reflect belief that the proxy is adequate. They may reflect the fact that acting against the proxy is costly, risky, or institutionally unrewarded. The actor who resists the proxy may bear immediate costs, while the costs of continuing the proxy are diffuse, delayed, or borne by others. This produces a familiar structure: the locally rational choice reproduces the globally inadequate system.

Goodhartization therefore arises not only from measurement error, but from a mismatch between the level at which rational action is rewarded and the level at which purpose is harmed. The proxy organizes local incentives, while the consequences of divergence accumulate at a broader institutional or social scale. This is why Goodhartization often persists even when many participants recognize it. Recognition does not change the incentive structure that makes continuation rational.

**5.5 From avoidance to institutional self-protection**

When reconnection-cost avoidance is repeated, the institution may begin to protect the proxy from reconsideration. This does not require explicit conspiracy or deliberate suppression. More often, protection occurs through ordinary administrative filters: concerns are delayed, translated into existing categories, treated as insufficiently general, or assigned to procedures that cannot alter the proxy itself.

Over time, the proxy becomes surrounded by stabilizing routines. Reports are built around it. Roles are defined in relation to it. Incentives are tied to it. Public accountability is expressed through it. Once this happens, the proxy becomes part of the institution's self-description. Questioning it no longer appears as a technical request for revision, but as a challenge to the institution's capacity to explain itself.

**5.6 Implications for Goodhartization**

Reconnection-cost avoidance explains why Goodhartization is durable. A proxy becomes Goodhartized not simply because it is selected poorly, optimized excessively, or misunderstood cognitively. It becomes Goodhartized when the institution repeatedly avoids the work required to test and revise its relation to the purpose by which it is justified.

This mechanism also clarifies why lowering one kind of cost is not enough. Making divergence visible does not guarantee revision. Improving measurement does not guarantee reconnection. Producing better evidence does not guarantee institutional uptake. If the costs of articulation, uptake, and revision remain high, or if they fall on actors without sufficient authority, the proxy may remain dominant.

The key issue is therefore not only whether a proxy is accurate, but whether the institution preserves a functioning pathway for reopening its adequacy. A proxy can remain useful only if it remains revisable. When revisability is lost, the proxy becomes an end in its own right. That is the institutional core of Goodhartization.

The concluding section returns to the contemporary significance of this argument. In AI-mediated institutions, proxy optimization can be intensified, but some reconnection costs may also be deliberately reduced. The decisive question is whether AI systems are designed merely to optimize existing proxies or to support the institutional work of reconnecting proxies to their purposes.

  


**6\. Conclusion: Proxy Optimization and the Work of Reconnection**

This paper has argued that Goodhartization should be understood not merely as the failure of a metric, but as the institutional stabilization of a proxy after its adequacy has become questionable. Proxies, metrics, and procedures are often necessary. They make complex purposes tractable under finite cognitive, organizational, and political capacity. The problem begins when such proxies become effective ends in their own right, while the institutional circuit that would reconnect them to their justifying purposes weakens.

The framework developed in this paper distinguishes Goodhartization as a phenomenon from reconnection failure as a proposed mechanism. Goodhartization names the process by which a proxy becomes stabilized as an object of action, evaluation, and justification. Reconnection failure names the weakening of the circuit through which proxy inadequacy is recognized, articulated, taken up, and made revisable. This distinction matters because many institutional failures are not sustained by ignorance alone. Actors may already know that the proxy is inadequate. What fails is the pathway from recognition to revision.

The paper has also argued that this pathway fails because reconnection is costly. Recognition requires attention to diffuse or contested purposes. Articulation can expose conflict and responsibility. Uptake requires institutional channels and authority. Revision can require redesigning criteria, altering incentives, redistributing responsibility, and accepting transitional uncertainty. These costs are not distributed evenly. As a result, institutions may continue relying on proxies not because those proxies remain adequate, but because the work of replacing or revising them is locally costly, politically exposed, and institutionally avoidable. This is not a story of bad actors or institutional failure in the exceptional sense. It is a story of how locally rational behavior under institutional pressure produces outcomes that no one explicitly chose.

This is why Goodhartization becomes self-sustaining. A proxy is not only maintained once. Its non-revision becomes precedential, normalized, and easier to repeat. The burden of proof shifts to those who demand revision. Alternative arrangements become less familiar. The proxy becomes part of the institution's self-description. In this way, what begins as a practical compression can become an institutional center of gravity.

This framework has particular significance for AI-mediated institutions. AI systems can intensify proxy optimization. They can make existing targets, rankings, classifications, and evaluative procedures faster, more scalable, and more pervasive. In this sense, AI can accelerate Goodhartization by strengthening the very proxies that institutions already find convenient to maintain. Without deliberate design, the default trajectory is proxy optimization: AI systems will improve performance against existing proxies while leaving the proxies themselves unquestioned.

Yet AI systems may also reduce some of the costs of reconnection, if deliberately designed to do so. They can help articulate discrepancies, summarize dispersed feedback, compare alternative criteria, generate revision proposals, and make institutional reconsideration easier to initiate. This does not happen automatically. A system optimized to improve performance against existing proxies will not, by itself, preserve the capacity to question those proxies. Reconnection-cost reduction requires design choices that keep proxy adequacy examinable and revisable. This paper does not offer a complete design program for AI-mediated institutions. It identifies the institutional function such design would need to support: preserving the capacity to reopen and revise proxies.

The implication is not that institutions should abandon proxies. That would be neither realistic nor desirable. Institutions need stable indicators, routines, and procedures in order to coordinate action and justify decisions. The task is instead to preserve the conditions under which proxies can be reopened, tested, and revised. A proxy can remain useful only if it remains revisable.

The problem is not that institutions use proxies. The problem is that they can lose the capacity to revise them.
